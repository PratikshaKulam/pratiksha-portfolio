Product Design Case Study

Rebuilding trust in a financial operations dashboard

An internal platform used by wealth-management and operations teams to track and process financial transactions. Conflicting data across backend systems meant users stopped trusting what they saw on screen — and started working around the product entirely.

01 — The problem & context

Users could see the data. They just didn't believe it.

Every morning, operations analysts opened this dashboard to answer one question: what needs my attention today? Pending trades, settled trades, and anything that had failed validation. Each row represented real money moving between institutions, so getting it wrong had downstream settlement consequences.

The data came from two different backend systems that updated on different schedules — a core transaction system and a separate validation layer. That timing gap created a specific, corrosive failure: the same trade could appear as "Completed" in one place and still be flagged as an error in another.

User cost

Manual double-checking

Analysts stopped trusting the queue and began verifying trades by hand — slowing the whole morning down.

Workaround

Shadow spreadsheets

Teams tracked exceptions in their own Excel files, outside the product entirely.

Business cost

Delayed resolution

Issues surfaced late, which pushed against downstream settlement timelines.

The clearest signal something was wrong was never technical. It was an ops lead messaging to say they couldn't tell which trades needed attention — which meant they couldn't start their day.

03 — Research & inputs

I stopped assuming it was a UI problem

My first instinct was that this was a sync or refresh issue on the front end. That turned out to be wrong — and finding that out changed the entire direction of the work.

What the benchmarking told me: a dedicated exception queue was a proven convention, so the risk there was low. But reconciling conflicting statuses into a single stated reason was something nobody did well — which is where the real opportunity was, and also where I'd need to be most careful about getting the precedence rule right.
🔍

The reframe: this was never a visual problem. The interface was faithfully showing raw data from two systems that disagreed. The design problem was deciding what the product should say when its own sources conflict.

04 — Key decisions & trade-offs

Three decisions, each with something given up

Decision 1 — Resolve conflicts to a single actionable status

CONSIDERED & REJECTED

Show both raw values and let users interpret. Most transparent — but it pushed the reconciliation work onto the user and was exactly what caused the distrust.

TRIED & INSUFFICIENT

Increase data refresh frequency. My first attempt. It narrowed the window but never closed it, because the two systems update on fundamentally different cycles.

CHOSEN

Derive one combined status at the UI layer, prioritising the validation flag over settlement status whenever the two disagree.

The trade-off: a trade that was seconds away from resolving could still briefly surface as "needs attention." I accepted that false-positive cost deliberately — in a settlement context, showing a problem that turns out to be fine is recoverable; hiding a real problem is not. I made the underlying values available on the record detail so nothing was hidden, just no longer competing for the user's judgement.

Decision 2 — Pull exceptions out of the main table into a dedicated queue

CONSIDERED & REJECTED

Highlight exception rows in place. Lower effort and kept one source of truth — but users were still scanning hundreds of rows to find them.

CHOSEN

A separate, prioritised exception queue, grouped by error type and open/closed state, with urgency surfaced first.

The trade-off: a second place to look risks fragmenting the workflow. I mitigated it by making the exception count visible from the main view and keeping a single record detail shared between both, so users never had to reconcile two versions of the same trade.

Decision 3 — Progressive disclosure, against user preference

TENSION

Ops wanted every field visible. They had built their confidence around seeing everything at once and pushed back on removing anything.

TENSION

Product wanted a clean, minimal view. New joiners were visibly overwhelmed by the existing density.

CHOSEN

Lead with the fields that drive the decision — status, urgency, ownership — and keep secondary detail one interaction away rather than removed.

The trade-off: I sat with each group separately to find which fields they genuinely used versus which they simply felt safer seeing. Some long-tenured users still preferred the old density, and I accepted that — most adapted after a few iterations, and nothing was actually taken away.

05 — Execution & artifacts

From raw conflicting data to a prioritised queue

Toggle between the original view and the redesign. The core shift is from "here is all the data" to "here is what needs you, and why."

workbench / transactions

States & edge cases I defined

Because a single unhandled state here means a missed trade, I specified behaviour for each case rather than leaving it to implementation.

Edge case

Conflicting statuses

Resolves to the actionable state; both raw values shown on the detail record.

Edge case

Missing status

Grouped into an explicit "Pending update" bucket so no record is invisible.

Edge case

Illogical date sequence

Settlement before trade date is flagged and routed out of the clean queue.

Edge case

SLA breach

Escalates to the top of the queue with explicit urgency treatment.

Edge case

Empty queue

Confirms "nothing needs attention" rather than showing an ambiguous blank state.

Edge case

Stale / loading data

Surfaces last-updated time so users know whether they're seeing current information.

Design system contributions

These patterns had to hold up across five views, so I built them as components rather than one-off screens.

STATUS TOKENS
Semantic, not decorative — one meaning per token, used identically everywhere.
DATA TABLE
Shared column logic, sorting, density and row-state behaviour across all five views.
STATE PATTERNS
Default, loading, empty, error and stale defined once and reused.
GUIDANCE
Contextual help pattern added after testing showed users knew what was wrong but not what to do.
🔧

A constraint I designed around: the grouping I wanted wasn't supported by the backend at the time. Rather than block the release, I worked with engineering on a UI-layer approach that delivered the value immediately while the proper data-side change was planned.

How I knew it actually worked

We measured it two ways — task performance in testing, and whether the behaviour that signalled distrust actually stopped.

"I don't need to keep my own spreadsheet anymore."
— Operations lead, after release. The clearest signal: the workaround people had built to compensate for the product simply stopped being necessary.

That one was the outcome I cared about most. Task time is useful, but people only abandon a workaround they've invested in when they genuinely trust the system to do the job instead.

07 — Reflection

What I'd do differently

I brought the ops team in too late. I did the research and the workflow mapping with them, but then went away and came back with something close to final. That turned what should have been a collaborative decision into a reaction — and it's why the pushback on density was sharper than it needed to be.

If I ran it again, I'd co-design the density and hierarchy decisions with the long-tenured users, in the room, at low fidelity. Not because they'd have designed it better, but because people defend a decision they helped shape and resist one that arrives finished.

The related lesson: my first instinct — that this was a refresh problem — was wrong, and I spent real time there. I've since become much faster at asking "is the data actually wrong, or is the product just repeating a disagreement it should be resolving?" before assuming the fix lives in the interface.
