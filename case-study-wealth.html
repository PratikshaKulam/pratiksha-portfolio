Wealth Operations Workbench · Product design

Users could see the data. They just didn't believe it.

An internal platform where wealth-management and operations teams track and process financial transactions. Two backend systems disagreed on screen — so people stopped trusting the product and started working around it.

Team

The problem & context

One trade. Two truths.

Every morning

“What needs my attention today?”

Operations analysts opened this dashboard to answer one question: which pending, settled or failed trades need them. Each row was real money moving between institutions, so getting it wrong had downstream settlement consequences.

The data came from two backend systems on different schedules — a core transaction system updated overnight in batches, and a validation layer updated through the day. That gap created a corrosive failure:

Core transaction system
settlement_status: Completed
trade_date: 03/14
Updated overnight, batch
CONFLICT
Validation layer
validation_flag: Error
resolution_status: Open
Updated incrementally, intraday

What it cost

User cost

Manual double-checking

Analysts stopped trusting the queue and verified trades by hand, slowing the whole morning down.

Workaround

Shadow spreadsheets

Teams tracked exceptions in their own Excel files, outside the product entirely.

Business cost

Delayed resolution

Issues surfaced late, pushing against downstream settlement timelines.

They couldn't tell which trades needed attention — so they couldn't start their day.— the signal that something was wrong was never technical; it was an ops lead's message

My role

Sole designer, owning the transaction workflow end to end.

From problem framing and user sessions with ops and compliance, through workflow mapping, IA, wireframes, prototypes and usability testing, to design-system ownership, edge-case definition and engineering walkthroughs. I was also the person ops leads messaged directly when something didn't match how they actually worked.

  • Product Manager — business requirements, prioritisation, release scope
  • Business Analyst — data definitions, field-level logic, validation rules
  • Engineering (EM + 5) — feasibility, data constraints, implementation
  • QA (2) — state and edge-case verification against designed behaviour
  • Ops & compliance — workflow observation, testing, ongoing feedback

A dedicated queue was a proven convention — low risk. But reconciling conflicting statuses into one stated reason was something nobody did well. That's where the opportunity was, and where the precedence rule had to be exactly right.

The reframe

This was never a visual problem. What should the product say when its own sources conflict?

The interface was faithfully showing raw data from two systems that disagreed. The design problem was the rule — so I made it explicit.

03

Key decisions & trade-offs

Three decisions, each with something given up.

Try the rule

The precedence rule: validation wins when the systems disagree.

Pick what each system reports and see the single status the product shows.

Core system · settlement_status
Validation layer · validation_flag

What the user sees

Needs attention

Raw source values stay on the record detail — nothing hidden, just no longer competing for judgement.

✕ Considered & rejected

Show both raw values and let users interpret. Most transparent — but it pushed reconciliation onto the user, exactly what caused the distrust.

◐ Tried & insufficient

Increase data refresh frequency. My first attempt. It narrowed the window but never closed it — the systems update on fundamentally different cycles.

✓ Chosen

Derive one combined status at the UI layer, prioritising the validation flag over settlement status whenever they disagree.

The trade-off I accepted

A trade seconds from resolving could briefly surface as “needs attention.”

In settlement, showing a problem that turns out fine is recoverable; hiding a real problem is not. I accepted the false-positive cost deliberately and kept the underlying values on the record detail.

✕ Considered & rejected

Highlight exception rows in place. Lower effort, one source of truth — but users were still scanning hundreds of rows to find them.

✓ Chosen

A separate, prioritised exception queue, grouped by error type and open/closed state, with urgency surfaced first.

The trade-off I accepted

A second place to look risks fragmenting the workflow.

I mitigated it by making the exception count visible from the main view and keeping one record detail shared between both — users never reconcile two versions of the same trade.

⇆ Tension

Ops wanted every field visible. They'd built their confidence around seeing everything at once and pushed back on removing anything.

⇆ Tension

Product wanted a clean, minimal view. New joiners were visibly overwhelmed by the existing density.

✓ Chosen

Lead with the fields that drive the decision — status, urgency, ownership — and keep secondary detail one interaction away, not removed.

The trade-off I accepted

Some long-tenured users still preferred the old density.

I sat with each group separately to find which fields they genuinely used versus which they felt safer seeing. Most adapted after a few iterations — and nothing was actually taken away.

Information architecture

Restructured around intent, not around the database.

“Needs attention” became the default landing view instead of a filter buried in an “all transactions” table — and every exception type resolves back to one shared record detail, so there's never a second version of the truth.

Design system

Built as components, because they had to hold across five views.

Status tokens
AttentionReviewPendingDone

Semantic, not decorative — one meaning per token, used identically everywhere.

Data table

Shared column logic, sorting, density and row-state behaviour across all five views.

State patterns

Default, loading, empty, error and stale — defined once and reused.

Guidance

A contextual help pattern added after testing showed users knew what was wrong but not what to do.

🔧

A constraint I designed around: the grouping I wanted wasn't supported by the backend at the time. Rather than block the release, I worked with engineering on a UI-layer approach that delivered the value immediately while the proper data-side change was planned.

05

Impact & outcomes

How I knew it actually worked.

Measured two ways: task performance in testing, and whether the behaviour that signalled distrust actually stopped.

Faster.

Time to identify and act on an exception dropped between testing rounds.

Fewer.

Errors and missed items in the second usability round, after the guidance redesign.

Lower.

Duplicate-handling and “missing trade” complaints from ops.

I don't need to keep my own spreadsheet anymore.— Operations lead, after release

That was the outcome I cared about most. Task time is useful, but people only abandon a workaround they've invested in when they genuinely trust the system to do the job instead.

Reflection

I brought the ops team in too late.

I did the research and workflow mapping with them, then went away and came back with something close to final. That turned a collaborative decision into a reaction — and it's why the pushback on density was sharper than it needed to be.

Next time I'd co-design density and hierarchy with long-tenured users, in the room, at low fidelity. Not because they'd design it better, but because people defend a decision they helped shape and resist one that arrives finished.

And my first instinct — that this was a refresh problem — cost real time. I'm now much faster at asking: is the data actually wrong, or is the product just repeating a disagreement it should be resolving?
